How to hire a full stack development company: 8 questions to ask before you sign

Every agency pitch deck promises a modern stack and a list of happy clients. None of them tells you who owns the code, who actually writes it, or whether they’ll still reply six months after launch. Here are eight questions to ask before you hire a full-stack development company and what a good answer sounds like.

By Vamsi Krishna20 November 2026full stack, development agency


You've got a product idea and a launch date, but no engineering team, and hiring one in-house would take months you don't have. So you start calling agencies. By the end of the week the pitch decks have blurred together, each one promising a modern stack and a list of happy clients.

No deck shows which partner will still reply to your messages six months after launch. You find that out by asking. Before you hire a full stack development company to build your product or MVP, ask these eight questions and pay attention to how each one is answered.

1. Who will own the code, designs, and data when the project ends?

Founders often skip this question and regret it later. Ownership of software doesn't move to you just because you paid for it. A Mondaq analysis for Indian founders notes that contractors and freelancers usually don't transfer IP rights to the company that hired them automatically, so a written assignment is essential. Morgan Lewis's note on service agreements explains that the traditional position is for the client to own what a supplier builds, because the client pays for the work, but the agreement still has to set out who owns what. Whatever you agree to, sign the IP agreement before development begins, not after.

Ownership also covers more than your own code. Most products use open-source packages and paid services. Ask for a list of both, with their licenses, so you know what you can change, sell, or move later.

A good answer sounds like, "Everything we build for you is assigned to you in writing once you pay: source code, designs, and documentation. Here's the clause. If we reuse any of our own libraries, here's the license you get to them."

2. Who will actually write the code?

The people in the pitch meeting are often not the people in your repository. Ask for names, roles, and how much of each person's week goes to your project. Ask whether any work is subcontracted. In 2026, also ask how the team uses AI coding tools and who reviews what those tools produce. If the team works in another time zone, ask how many working hours overlap with yours each day.

A good answer sounds like, "These three people will build it. You'll talk to the lead every week. We use AI tools for first drafts, and a senior engineer reviews every change before it merges."

3. How do you handle changes to scope?

Your scope will change. You'll learn things from your first users that nobody could have planned for. What matters is whether a change becomes a short conversation or a surprise invoice.

A good answer sounds like, "Changes go into a written request with a price and a time impact, and nothing starts until you approve it. You own the backlog and decide what gets cut to make room."

4. What does "done" mean for each milestone?

"Done" should never mean "the developer says so." Each milestone needs acceptance criteria you agree on before the work starts and a working version you can click through yourself. Tie payments to those checkpoints, not to dates on a calendar. Weekly demos are even better: problems surface while they're still cheap to fix.

A good answer sounds like, "Every milestone ends with a demo on a staging site and a checklist we agreed on at the start. You sign it off, then we invoice."

5. How do you protect security and user data?

A data leak in month two can end a young company. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report puts the global average cost of a breach at USD 4.44 million. Ask how the team guards against common web attacks, how they store passwords and API keys, and who can reach production. If you'll handle personal data from users in India, ask how they'll help you meet the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.

A good answer sounds like a specific list. Role-based access, secrets kept out of the code, dependencies scanned for known flaws, and personal data you don't need never collected in the first place. If all you hear is "Don't worry, security is covered," keep asking.

6. What exactly do we get at handover?

Partners differ most at handover. Some send a zip file and stop replying. Others leave you able to run, fix and extend the product without them. Ask about handover in the first meeting, not in the last week of the project.

A good answer sounds like, "You get admin access to the code repository from day one. Hosting, domains, and app store accounts sit under your company, not ours. At handover you get setup documentation, a runbook for routine tasks, and a walkthrough with whoever maintains it next."

7. What happens after launch?

Bugs show up after launch, because that's when real people start using the product. Ask how long fixes are covered at no extra cost, how fast the team responds when something breaks, and what ongoing support costs. Ask, too, what happens if you later move the work to your own team or another vendor. If a partner makes leaving difficult, that tells you how they'll treat you while you stay.

A good answer sounds like, "Launch bugs are fixed free for an agreed period. After that, there's a support plan with stated response times, or we hand over cleanly and help your new team get started."

8. Can I speak to a client whose product is still live?

The agency writes its own case studies, so a ten-minute call with a past client will tell you more. Ask to speak with someone whose product launched at least a year ago, so you hear about maintenance and support as well as the build. Ask that client what went wrong and how the team dealt with it. Every project has a bad week, and you want to know how this partner handled theirs.

A good answer sounds like, "Here are two clients you can call. One launched last year and is still with us; the other took the work in-house and can tell you how the handover went."

Red flags when you hire a full stack development company

Walk away, or at least slow down, if you notice any of these:

  • They won't put IP assignment in writing, or they want to host your product in their own accounts.

  • They quote a fixed price after one call, without asking about your users or your data.

  • The senior people disappear after the contract is signed.

  • You don't see working software until the end of the project.

  • They can't explain what handover includes.

  • They push you to sign quickly with a "this week only" discount.

  • They get vague when you ask who reviews AI-generated code.

Whoever you choose, pay closest attention to the answers on IP, timelines, and support.
